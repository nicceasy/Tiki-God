# A History of Tiki

*From the punch bowl to the Zombie, the Mai Tai, Polynesian Pop, the revival, and today's arguments about all of it.*

This document is the project's history reference. It explains where tiki drinks came from, who made them, why they took the shapes they did, and how the culture around them has changed. The drink-making ideas behind the recipes are covered separately in [`concepts.md`](concepts.md). Key dates are listed in [`timeline.json`](timeline.json).

**Conventions.** Bracketed numbers such as [12] point to the numbered **Sources** list at the end. Where sources disagree, a **Contested** note says so and gives the competing claims. A **Verification notes** section at the end lists the claims that rest only on printed secondary literature and were not re-checked online while this was written.

## 0. What "tiki" is (and isn't)

Tiki is an American invention. It began in Depression-era Hollywood, when a young Texan drifter took drinks from the Caribbean, dressed them up with South Seas props, and served them in a dim room that felt far from Los Angeles [1][2]. Nothing in a tiki bar is "authentic" to any single place. The drinks come mostly from the Caribbean rum tradition. The décor mixes Hawaiian, Marquesan, Māori, Tahitian and invented Pacific imagery. The food began as Cantonese-American. The music came from Hollywood studios and Honolulu hotel lounges [35][44].

The word *tiki* itself comes from Polynesian languages. In Māori and Marquesan tradition, Tiki is the first man, and the word also names carved human figures, such as the Māori *hei-tiki* pendant. The Hawaiian cognate is *kiʻi*. The carved "tiki god" of American bars blends these sources with pure invention, and it was not part of the earliest 1930s bars. The name "tiki" for the whole style took hold in the 1950s and was applied to the style's history afterwards, largely by collectors in the 1990s [44][35].

Historians usually divide the story into five overlapping eras:

| Era | Approx. dates | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Roots | 1600s–1933 | Punch, grog, Caribbean rum, Planter's Punch, Havana's cocktail bars |
| Golden age (Don & Vic) | 1933–1959 | The founding bars; the Zombie and the Mai Tai; chains spread |
| Polynesian Pop | c. 1950–1970 | A mass-culture craze: architecture, music, mugs, motels, Disney, Hawaii statehood |
| Decline | c. 1968–1990 | Premixes, sour mix, closures; tiki drinks become "umbrella drinks" |
| Revival and reckoning | 1990s–present | Collectors, historians, craft bartenders; then debates over appropriation and a "tropical" reframing |

## 1. Roots

### 1.1 Punch: the first "exotic" mixed drink

Every tiki drink descends from **punch**. The earliest known written mention in English is in a letter of **28 September 1632**. Robert Addams, an East India Company employee in India, hoped his colleagues would "drincke punch" [31][46]. The drink was a mix of spirit (at first usually arrack, a distillate of palm sap or rice), citrus, sugar, water and spice. English merchants and sailors in Asia made it and carried it home. It became the sociable drink of the 17th and 18th centuries, served from a shared bowl [46].

> **Contested: the name.** The usual story traces "punch" to the Hindi/Sanskrit *pañc* ("five"), for the drink's five ingredients [31]. David Wondrich and other historians treat this as plausible but unproven and note rival derivations, such as *puncheon*, a cask [46]. Treat "five ingredients" as a handy memory aid rather than settled etymology.

Punch set the grammar that tiki later used:
- strong spirit balanced by sour citrus and sugar;
- enough water or ice to make it drinkable in quantity;
- spice as a finishing layer;
- a communal serving vessel with a sense of ceremony.

### 1.2 Grog and the Royal Navy

Once England took **Jamaica in 1655**, rum gradually replaced beer and brandy as the sailors' spirit ration [30]. Neat rum in large daily amounts caused discipline problems. On **21 August 1740**, Vice Admiral **Edward Vernon** ordered in his *Captain's Order No. 349* that the ration be mixed with water (commonly given as four parts water to one of rum) and issued in two servings a day. Sailors who could afford it were allowed sugar and limes to make the mix more pleasant [30]. Vernon's nickname, "Old Grog," came from his coat of *grogram* cloth, and the drink took his name [30].

Grog matters to tiki in three ways:
- It fixed **rum + water + lime + sugar** as the working sailor's drink across the British Caribbean.
- It linked rum to the navy and the sea, an image later cultivated by "Navy" rums and Don the Beachcomber's **Navy Grog** (c. 1941).
- The navy's blended rums, heavy with Guyanese Demerara and Jamaican pot-still rum, set a style that tiki bartenders later reached for when they wanted dark, rich, strong rum. The Royal Navy's daily rum issue ended on **31 July 1970** ("Black Tot Day") [30][47].

> Note: the link between citrus and scurvy prevention was not properly understood in 1740. Vernon's limes were for flavor. The Navy's systematic use of lemon (and later lime) juice against scurvy came decades later [30][47].

### 1.3 Rum country: the Caribbean styles

Tiki is, at bottom, a way of combining rums, so the Caribbean's regional styles are the palette. Each style follows from its colonial history: who owned the island, what stills they used, and what sugar product they fermented [47][43].

| Style (region) | Raw material and still | Flavor role in tiki | Historical notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jamaican** | Molasses; long, "dirty" fermentations (dunder, muck); double-retort **pot stills** | "Funk" (the Jamaican *hogo*): high esters, overripe fruit and banana; body and power | Among the oldest rum-producing islands (Appleton dates itself to 1749; J. Wray & Nephew was founded in 1825). Jamaican rum was the backbone of Planter's Punch and of Vic's first Mai Tai [43][47] |
| **Barbadian** | Molasses; mix of pot and column stills | Balanced, rounded, lightly fruity and oaky; a "bridge" rum | Barbados is often called rum's birthplace. Mid-17th-century accounts describe "kill-devil" or "rumbullion." Mount Gay traces itself to a 1703 deed [47] |
| **Guyanese ("Demerara")** | Molasses (Demerara sugar region); historic wooden pot and Coffey stills now at Diamond distillery (Port Mourant, Versailles, Enmore) | Rich, dark, burnt-sugar, smoky; the classic **151 overproof** | Central to British Navy blends; Lemon Hart 151 (a Demerara) was Donn Beach's favorite overproof accent [8][43] |
| **French Caribbean (Martinique, Guadeloupe)** | Fresh **sugarcane juice** (*rhum agricole*), column stills | Grassy, vegetal, dry, "green" | Agricole production grew after the late-19th-century sugar-price collapse. Martinique's AOC dates from 1996. Rhum St. James (Martinique) went into Vic's post-war Mai Tai blend [22][23][43] |
| **Spanish Caribbean (Cuba, Puerto Rico)** | Molasses; column stills; charcoal filtration; light aging | Light, crisp, dry backbone; lets citrus show | Facundo Bacardí Massó founded Bacardi in Santiago de Cuba in 1862 and pioneered the lighter *ron ligero* style. Havana Club followed in 1934. Puerto Rico's industry (Serrallés/Don Q and later Bacardi) grew after Repeal. "Gold Puerto Rican rum" is the light partner in Don's Zombie [8][47] |

Other islands matter too: Haiti (Barbancourt, cane-juice based), Trinidad (Angostura's rums and bitters), the Virgin Islands (Cruzan) and later Hawaii. But the five groups above supplied almost every rum named in the classic tiki recipes.

### 1.4 Planter's Punch and its rhyme

Out of punch and grog came **Planter's Punch**, the Jamaican plantation drink of rum, lime, sugar and water, often seasoned with bitters and nutmeg. Two printed verses show its formula:

- **1878 (London *Fun* magazine):** a rhyming West Indian recipe. Fill a wineglass once with lemon juice and twice with sugar, add three glasses of rum and four of cold water, "so they say in Jamaica" [32].
- **8 August 1908 (*New York Times*):** the better-known verse, "This recipe I give to thee, / Dear brother in the heat". It calls for *two* of sour (lime), *one and a half* of sweet, *three* of strong ("Old Jamaica") and *four* of weak [32].

Later retellings smoothed these into the mnemonic **"one of sour, two of sweet, three of strong, four of weak."** No single original text uses exactly that wording. The parts are counted by volume, and "weak" means water or ice melt.

> **Contested: "first appearance."** Some sources call the 1908 NYT verse the first print reference to Planter's Punch. Others point to the 1878 *Fun* verse, and some cite an NYT recipe from the 1870s [32]. The safest summary: the drink existed in Jamaican planter culture by the later 19th century, and rhyming ratio-recipes circulated from at least 1878.

The rhyme matters to this project because it is the oldest statement of the **sour : sweet : strong : weak** balance that every tiki recipe still works within. Don the Beachcomber's early menu consisted largely of elaborate variations on a Planter's Punch [40][43].

### 1.5 Havana's golden age and Prohibition travel

National Prohibition in the United States ran from **17 January 1920** until Repeal on **5 December 1933**. It sent American drinkers, and many American bartenders, south. Havana advertised itself as an easy, glamorous place to drink. U.S. visitors to Cuba rose from about 33,000 in 1914 to about 90,000 by 1928. Pan American Airways started its Key West–Havana route in the late 1920s, and the city reportedly had thousands of bars [34].

The central figure was **Constantino Ribalaigua Vert** ("Constante"), a Catalan-born bartender. He was working at **El Floridita** on Obispo Street by **1914**, had bought the bar by **1918**, and became known as *el rey de los coteleros*, "the cocktail king" [33]. He is credited with a large family of **Daiquiri** variations (numbered Daiquirís with maraschino, grapefruit or curaçao) and with popularizing the **frozen**, blender-made daiquiri in the early 1930s. The best-known customer was Ernest Hemingway, whose sugarless, grapefruit-and-maraschino "Papa Doble" came from Constante's bar [33]. Other Havana landmarks included **Sloppy Joe's**, José García's bar, opened c. 1919, and the hotel bars staffed by Americans who had fled Prohibition [34].

Havana showed tiki two things:
1. Light rum, lime and sugar could be refined into elegant, precisely balanced drinks.
2. A bar trip could itself be the reason for travel.

Donn Beach's tropical drinks are, in effect, Havana's precision applied to Jamaican-style punch. Trader Vic went to Havana in 1937 specifically to learn from its bars [18].

### 1.6 The South Seas in the American imagination (pre-1934)

The dream of the Pacific was already in place before Don's bar opened:
- **Hawaiian music** swept the mainland after Hawaii's pavilion at the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco.
- The **Royal Hawaiian Hotel**, Waikiki's "Pink Palace" built for Matson Line passengers, opened in 1927.
- Hollywood made a string of South Seas romances (for example *White Shadows in the South Seas*, 1928, and *Tabu*, 1931), with *Mutiny on the Bounty* following in 1935.

Don the Beachcomber gave a movie-going city a place to step inside that fantasy with a drink in hand [35][44].

## 2. Donn Beach and Don the Beachcomber

### 2.1 The drifter

**Ernest Raymond Beaumont Gantt** was born on **22 February 1907** in Limestone County, Texas (Farrar is usually given) [1]. Accounts of his youth mention time in New Orleans and a grandfather who taught him about rum and, allegedly, bootlegging [1]. In **1926** he left home to see the world. He worked his way around the Caribbean and the South Pacific, a journey he later told as the adventures of a beachcomber [1]. By the early 1930s he was in Los Angeles. He reportedly got by on odd jobs, some bootlegging, and lending his collection of Pacific curios to film studios as set dressing [1][2].

### 2.2 Don's Beachcomber Café

With Prohibition ending, Gantt opened a small bar in the **McCadden Hotel at 1722 North McCadden Place, Hollywood**. Accounts describe it as tiny, with about 25 seats [2]. It was decorated with nets, flotsam, bamboo and island souvenirs, and served rum drinks at a time when most American bars poured whiskey and gin. He called it **Don's Beachcomber Café** and began calling himself "Don the Beachcomber" [1][3].

> **Contested: 1933 or 1934?** Wikipedia and Alta give **1933**, and Alta says the bar opened "officially" on **5 December 1933**, Repeal Day [1][3]. Jeff Berry's earlier books and many articles (Punch, for example) date it to **1934** [2][40]. The difference probably comes from a soft opening in late 1933 versus the bar's first full year and first menu. In this project, write "1933/34" or "1934" and note the disagreement.

In **1937** he moved **across the street to 1727 North McCadden Place**. The new space was much larger, took the name **Don the Beachcomber**, and added **Cantonese food** [1][3]. The Chinese-American menu (spareribs, rumaki-style pupus, fried rice) became half of the "Polynesian restaurant" formula every imitator copied.

The room was a stage set. Light was kept low. Walls were covered in bamboo, lauhala matting, fishing nets and floats. Menus promised **"Rhum Rhapsodies."** Donn is widely credited with a sprinkler rigged to drip on the tin roof so guests heard tropical rain on demand, and with the idea that the bar should let people forget the city outside. A motto often attributed to him runs roughly "if you can't get to paradise, I'll bring it to you" [2][4][44].

Hollywood clientele made the place famous. Charlie Chaplin, Marlene Dietrich, Clark Gable, Howard Hughes and others appear in its lore, though the lists vary between sources and the details are hard to confirm [2][4].

### 2.3 The drinks: Donn's method

Donn's menu grew to dozens of original drinks. Among the famous ones:
- **Sumatra Kula** and **Vicious Virgin**, among the early drinks;
- the **Zombie** (1934);
- the **Missionary's Downfall**;
- the **Navy Grog** (c. 1941);
- the **Test Pilot**, **Cobra's Fang**, **Three Dots and a Dash** and **Pearl Diver**;
- the **Nui Nui** (originally called the *Pupule*) [15];
- the **Q.B. Cooler**.

Berry, whose decodings are the main modern source, describes a consistent method. Donn started from the Planter's Punch skeleton of rum, lime, sugar and water, then multiplied each part [40][43]:
- **Several rums in one drink.** A crisp Cuban or Puerto Rican rum went in for backbone, a pungent Jamaican for body and funk, and often a dash of **151-proof Demerara** for depth and heat. The line *"What one rum can't do, three rums can"* is widely attributed to Donn [17]. We found it only as a later attribution, not in a primary source, so treat it as a summary of his practice rather than a documented quote.
- **Several sours.** He paired lime with **grapefruit** (a Donn signature) or with orange or pineapple.
- **Several sweeteners and spices, each in small amounts:** honey, falernum, cinnamon syrup, vanilla, allspice liqueur (pimento dram), grenadine and maraschino.
- **Seasoning by the drop:** Angostura bitters and a few drops of Pernod or Herbsaint.
- **Technique:** Donn used electric **spindle drink mixers** to "flash blend" drinks with crushed ice for a few seconds, aerating and diluting them without turning them to slush. The whole contents then went into the glass [16][43].

The result was a drink whose flavor no one could reverse-engineer. That was deliberate.

### 2.4 Secrecy and the code

Donn guarded his recipes like trade secrets, since competitors were copying him within a few years. Berry's research documents the precautions [8][12][13][14]:
- Bartenders worked from recipes written in **code**.
- Syrups and spice blends were **pre-mixed** away from the bar and supplied in bottles marked only with numbers or labels such as **"Don's Mix," "Spices #2," "Spices #4"** and **"Gardenia Mix."**
- A bartender could therefore make a Zombie every night without knowing what was in it.

Decades of work by Berry decoded the main mixes as follows:

| Code name | Decoded contents (per Berry) | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| **Don's Mix** | 2 parts grapefruit juice to 1 part cinnamon syrup | Zombie (1934), Donga Punch [13] |
| **Spices #4** | Cinnamon syrup | Nui Nui and others [14][15] |
| **Spices #2** | Equal parts vanilla syrup and allspice liqueur (pimento dram) | Nui Nui and others [14] |
| **Gardenia Mix** | Butter, honey, and spices (cinnamon syrup, allspice liqueur, vanilla) | Pearl Diver [12][14] |

Much of Donn's bar staff were **Filipino-American bartenders**, and they carried the craft into the next generation of bars. **Ray Buhen**, one of Donn's early bartenders, later opened the **Tiki-Ti** in Los Angeles in 1961. **Mariano Licudine** became head bartender at Fort Lauderdale's **Mai-Kai** in 1956 [40]. The staff's recipe notebooks later became the most valuable sources for historians.

The best-known notebook belonged to **Dick Santiago**, a longtime Beachcomber employee hired in 1937 who stayed about fifteen years. In **2005** his daughter Jennifer brought Berry the pocket notebook of recipes her father had kept. Some drinks in it had been renamed or cut from the menu by 1940, which dates it to the late 1930s. Its Zombie was marked "old," which suggested it was the **original 1934 formula** [8][9]. The 1934 Zombie appeared in print for the first time in Berry's ***Sippin' Safari* (2007)**. The last puzzle, "Don's Mix," was solved as grapefruit juice with cinnamon syrup [8][9][13].

### 2.5 The Zombie

The **Zombie** first appeared in **late 1934**. According to the legend, Donn made it for a hungover customer who needed to get through a business meeting. The man came back days later complaining that the drink had turned him into the walking dead [10]. Don's restaurants **limited guests to two Zombies**, which added to the drink's reputation [10].

Its fame went national in **1939**. A copy was served at the **Hurricane Bar** at the **New York World's Fair**, run by the impresario **Monte Proser**, and "Zombie" became a household word, attached to many unrelated recipes [10]. The 1934 formula decoded by Berry uses:
- three rums: gold Puerto Rican, gold Jamaican and Lemon Hart 151 Demerara;
- lime juice, Don's Mix, falernum and grenadine;
- Angostura and a few drops of Pernod.

It is flash-blended and served in a tall "chimney" glass with a mint sprig [8]. Donn himself revised the recipe several times over the decades [8][58].

> **Contested: authorship.** Donn's authorship is widely accepted, but some researchers have found earlier uses of "Zombie" for drinks and noted rival claims, including a Chicago theory [11]. Whatever the name's history, the three-rum Beachcomber formula is Donn's.

### 2.6 Sunny Sund and the first themed chain

In **1937** Donn married **Cora Irene "Sunny" Sund**, a Minnesota-born waitress with a strong head for business [5]. Accounts agree that Donn was the creative force and Sunny the operator [5]. They **divorced in 1940**. Sunny kept the rights to the Don the Beachcomber name and concept **in the mainland United States**, and she built it into a chain of **about 16 locations**, often called the first themed restaurant chain in the U.S. [5]. Chicago was among the first branches outside Hollywood.

### 2.7 War service

During **World War II**, Gantt served in the **U.S. Army Air Forces** and rose to **lieutenant colonel** [6]. He received a **Purple Heart** for wounds suffered when a U-boat attacked his ship. Later, at the request of his friend **Gen. Jimmy Doolittle**, he ran **officers' rest-and-recreation camps** for airmen of the 12th and 15th Air Forces on **Capri**, the **French Riviera** (Nice and Cannes), **Venice and the Lido**, and **Sorrento**. That work earned him a **Bronze Star** [6]. Some drink names, such as the **Test Pilot** and the **Q.B. Cooler** (named after the "Quiet Birdmen," an aviators' fraternity), reflect his aviation connections [6].

### 2.8 Hawaii

Barred by the divorce settlement from running Beachcombers on the mainland, **Donn moved to Hawaii** after the war. He opened a Don the Beachcomber in Waikiki, usually dated to the later 1940s. In **1956** he created the **International Market Place** on Kalākaua Avenue on land leased from the Queen Emma Foundation. It was an open-air cluster of thatched huts, shops and bars built around a huge banyan tree, and Donn kept an office in a **treehouse** in its branches [7]. His Beachcomber there had a "Dagger Bar" lined with his blade collection. Martin Denny and Don Ho were among the musicians who played the Market Place [7]. Hawaii's tourism industry recognized Donn as a promoter of Waikiki [7].

Donn legally took the name **Donn Beach**, kept on with ventures around the Pacific, and died in Honolulu on **7 June 1989** [1]. His widow, **Phoebe Beach**, co-wrote a 2001 book of his recipes that also restated his Mai Tai claim (§3.5) [50].

## 3. Trader Vic

### 3.1 Victor Bergeron and Hinky Dink's

**Victor Jules Bergeron Jr.** (1902–1984) was born in San Francisco and grew up in the family grocery business in Oakland [18]. He lost a leg to illness as a child. As "Trader Vic" he later enjoyed telling customers it had been taken by a shark, and he sometimes stuck an ice pick into the wooden leg to prove the point [18][49].

On **17 November 1934**, with $500 of borrowed money, he opened **Hinky Dink's**, a modest bar at **San Pablo Avenue and 65th Street** in Oakland's Golden Gate district, decorated in a rustic, snowshoes-on-the-wall style [18][19].

### 3.2 The 1937 conversion

In **1937** Bergeron went to **Havana**, drank at the Floridita and other bars, and on the way home visited **Don the Beachcomber** in Hollywood [18][19]. He came back with a plan. He redecorated Hinky Dink's in a South Seas theme, built a menu of rum drinks, adopted the persona of a Pacific trader, and renamed the bar **Trader Vic's** [18].

> **Contested (minor): the renaming date.** Most sources say **1937**; some say 1937–38 [18][20].

Vic freely admitted he learned from Donn. Their styles differed:
- **Donn** went for complexity and secrecy.
- **Vic** was a practical restaurateur and a showman who liked **simpler, reproducible drinks**, often built around one excellent rum and one or two special syrups.

He also published his recipes (§3.6). Vic's house drinks included the **Scorpion** and its communal Scorpion Bowl, the **Fog Cutter**, the **Samoan Fog Cutter**, the **Tortuga**, and a **Navy Grog** of his own. The bowls and the fire and flourishes around them made Trader Vic's a place for groups and celebrations [49].

### 3.3 The Mai Tai (1944)

In **1944**, according to Vic's own account, he wanted a new drink to show off a fine old rum. He took a bottle of **17-year-old J. Wray & Nephew** Jamaican rum and mixed it with:
- fresh lime juice;
- **orange curaçao** (Holland DeKuyper);
- **French orgeat** (Garnier);
- a little **rock candy syrup**.

He shook it with shaved ice and served it in a large glass with a spent lime shell and a mint sprig [21][22][36]. The first people to try it were friends visiting from Tahiti, **Ham and Carrie Guild**. On tasting it, Carrie is said to have exclaimed ***"Maita'i roa ae!"***, Tahitian for roughly "very good" or "out of this world, the best," and the drink was named after her words [21][22].

The Mai Tai was essentially a **rum sour sweetened with orange liqueur and almond**, a refinement of the Daiquiri and Planter's Punch lines. Its restraint was its genius. The 1944 recipe has five ingredients and no fruit juice other than lime.

### 3.4 The rum problem

The Mai Tai's success used up its rum. The 17-year Wray & Nephew was scarce, and as supplies ran out Vic reformulated:
- first with the **15-year** Wray & Nephew;
- then with **blends**, notably a Jamaican rum paired with a **Martinique rum** (Rhum St. James was often named) to imitate the old rum's weight and complexity [22][23].

The original Wray & Nephew 17 never came back. In 2004, an inventory found remnants of a barrel and a handful of unmarked bottles; one bottle's value was estimated at tens of thousands of dollars [22]. Appleton Estate later issued a limited "17 Year Old Legend" meant to recall it (see [`concepts.md`](concepts.md)).

This "rum problem" shaped modern practice. Because the original rum was irreplaceable, bartenders learned to **blend rums** to rebuild a lost profile. The Smuggler's Cove rum categories (see [`concepts.md`](concepts.md)) are a direct heir of that habit [43].

### 3.5 The Mai Tai dispute

The Mai Tai became the world's most famous tiki drink, so its origin became contested.

> **Contested: who invented the Mai Tai?**
> - **Trader Vic (1944, Oakland):** Vic's detailed account, supported by a signed statement from Carrie Guild, is the version most historians accept [21][23][24]. Vic put it memorably: anyone who said he didn't create the drink was "a dirty stinker" [24][49].
> - **Donn Beach (1933, Hollywood):** Donn claimed to have served a "Mai Tai Swizzle" in 1933. The claim was restated in the 2001 book by his widow Phoebe Beach and Arnold Bitner [24][50]. Donn's drink shares little with Vic's beyond the name. A longtime associate said Donn's real grievance was that Vic's Mai Tai resembled his **Q.B. Cooler** [24][26].
> - **The 1970 settlement:** The dispute reached the courts after **Sun-Vac Corporation** licensed the Don the Beachcomber name for a line of bottled mixes that included a "Mai Tai." Vic sued, presented evidence including statements from the Guilds, and the case was **settled out of court in Vic's favor** [23][27]. Some summaries wrongly describe it as a court ruling or as Donn suing Vic; the better-sourced version is a settlement with the mix company. Vic set out his side in a published statement, and the 1972 revision of his *Bartender's Guide* repeats it [23][49].
>
> The consensus today, reflected by Berry, Cate and most historians, is that **Vic created the Mai Tai as we know it**, while acknowledging that Vic learned the grammar of tropical drinks at Donn's bar [40][43].

### 3.6 The Royal Hawaiian Mai Tai (1953)

In **1953** the **Matson Navigation Company**, which owned Waikiki's Royal Hawaiian and Moana hotels, hired Vic to create the drink menus for its hotel bars, and the Mai Tai came to Hawaii [23][28]. There it became *the* island drink, and island bars reworked it into a longer, juicier drink with **pineapple and orange juice**, often with a **float of dark rum**.

> **Contested: did the 1953 Royal Hawaiian Mai Tai contain pineapple juice?** Many sources, including the hotel's own marketing, say Vic's 1953 Hawaiian recipe swapped some lime for pineapple and orange juice [28]. Kevin Crossman's research at *The Search for the Ultimate Mai Tai*, based on period documents, argues that the **1953 recipe contained no pineapple juice**. On his reading, Vic never added pineapple to a Mai Tai, and the juicy "island Mai Tai" developed through the 1960s, though the Royal Hawaiian and Moana kept closer to the original into the early 1970s [23]. This project's recipe catalog should keep the 1944 and "Royal Hawaiian/island" versions as separate records.

### 3.7 Books, chain and bottles

Vic shaped the home bar as much as the restaurant:
- ***Trader Vic's Book of Food and Drink* (1946)** [29][48];
- ***Trader Vic's Bartender's Guide* (1947)**, a thorough general bartending manual with tropical drinks alongside the classics;
- ***Trader Vic's Bartender's Guide, Revised* (1972)**, which updated the recipes and restated the Mai Tai story [29][49].

The chain grew through hotel partnerships [18]:
- **1949:** Western Hotels' Edward Carlson persuaded him to open the **Outrigger** in Seattle's Benjamin Franklin Hotel. It later expanded and was renamed Trader Vic's.
- **1951:** San Francisco, at **20 Cosmo Place**. It became a society landmark, and a quip often attributed to columnist Herb Caen held that the best restaurant in San Francisco was in Oakland.
- **1955:** **Conrad Hilton** opened a Trader Vic's in the **Beverly Hilton**.
- **1957:** the **Palmer House** in Chicago, followed by a licensing deal that took the brand across Hilton's hotels in North America, Europe, Asia and the Middle East.
- **1972:** the Oakland original moved to **Emeryville**, on the waterfront [20].

Vic also sold **bottled products**: Mai Tai mix, rock candy syrup, orgeat and other syrups, and later rums under his name. They put "Trader Vic's" in supermarkets and home bars. They also helped, though Vic surely didn't intend it, to replace fresh-juice drinks with **pre-mixes**, the practice later blamed for the decline of tiki drinks (§5).

Victor Bergeron died on **11 October 1984** [18]. Trader Vic's restaurants still operate, mostly outside the United States.

## 4. Polynesian Pop (c. 1945–1970)

Donn and Vic started tiki. **Polynesian Pop**, Sven Kirsten's term, was the mass movement that followed. For about twenty years, a South Seas fantasy shaped American restaurants, home décor, music, architecture and suburban parties [44][45].

### 4.1 Why the Pacific, why then?

- **Returning servicemen.** Millions of Americans served in the Pacific in WWII. Many had seen Hawaii, Samoa, the Solomons and the Philippines, and came home with souvenirs, stories and an idealized memory of island life, whatever the war's horrors [35][44].
- **Michener.** James A. Michener's ***Tales of the South Pacific* (1947)** won the 1948 Pulitzer Prize. Rodgers and Hammerstein adapted it as the Broadway musical ***South Pacific*** (opened **1949**), filmed in 1958 [57].
- **Kon-Tiki.** In **1947** the Norwegian explorer **Thor Heyerdahl** and five companions sailed the balsa raft *Kon-Tiki* about 101 days from Peru to the Tuamotus to test a theory of American settlement of Polynesia. Modern genetics and archaeology have largely rejected that theory, but the voyage became a sensation. The book (Norwegian 1948, **English 1950**) was a worldwide bestseller, and Heyerdahl's **documentary film (1950)** won an Academy Award. The name "Kon-Tiki" became shorthand for adventure in the South Seas, and a restaurant chain later borrowed it [56][44].
- **Hawaii statehood and the jet age.** Hawaii became the 50th state on **21 August 1959**. The same year, jet service cut the trip from the West Coast to a few hours. Mass tourism followed, and with it a Waikiki cocktail culture of resort drinks [35].
- **Postwar prosperity and the suburbs.** A growing middle class had money for themed nights out and backyard **luaus**. The tiki bar gave a whiskey-and-martini culture a harmless, exotic kind of fun.

### 4.2 Hawaii's bartenders and resort drinks

As Waikiki boomed, its hotel bars developed a **fruitier, showier, more photogenic** style made for tourists:
- **Harry K. Yee**, long-time head bartender at the Hilton Hawaiian Village (formerly Henry J. Kaiser's Hawaiian Village), created the **Blue Hawaii (1957)** for the Bols company to promote its blue curaçao. He also invented playful drinks such as the **Tropical Itch**, served with a bamboo back-scratcher. He is widely credited with popularizing the **paper parasol**, the **orchid garnish** and the **sugar-cane stick**. The umbrella claim has rivals, since umbrellas already appear in earlier Americana [40].
- The **Royal Hawaiian Mai Tai** (§3.6) spread through Honolulu and then the cruise lines.

This resort line of **juicy, sweet, colorful** drinks, served with a pineapple spear, cherry, orchid and umbrella, is what most people still picture when they hear "tiki."

### 4.3 The great temples

Across the country, entrepreneurs built ambitious Polynesian palaces, many run by former Beachcomber or Trader Vic's staff:

- **Tonga Room (1945, San Francisco).** Built in the Fairmont Hotel's former indoor swimming pool. The pool became a lagoon, a house band played on a floating barge, and simulated thunderstorms rolled through on a schedule. It still operates [35].
- **The Luau (1953, Beverly Hills).** Opened by the actor and restaurateur **Stephen Crane**, Lana Turner's former husband. In the late 1950s Crane expanded into **Kon-Tiki** restaurants in Sheraton hotels across North America, one of the biggest Polynesian chains of the era [44].
- **Mai-Kai (opened 28 December 1956, Fort Lauderdale).** Brothers **Bob and Jack Thornton** built a sprawling Polynesian restaurant with gardens, waterfalls and a nightly **Polynesian dance revue**. Its bar, run by ex-Beachcomber **Mariano Licudine**, preserved Don-style recipes for decades. Its **"Mystery Drink"** was delivered with a gong and a dancer [58]. The Mai-Kai was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It closed after a **2020 roof collapse** and reopened after a major restoration in the mid-2020s (see Verification notes).
- **Kahiki Supper Club (1961, Columbus, Ohio).** A vast A-frame "war canoe" of a building with giant tiki fireplaces, an indoor rain forest and live birds. It closed in **2000** and was demolished, a symbolic loss for revival-era fans [44].
- **Tiki-Ti (1961, Los Angeles).** A tiny bar on Sunset Boulevard opened by **Ray Buhen**, one of Donn's original bartenders. It is still run by his family and preserves a direct line to Beachcomber-era technique [40].
- Other notable names include San Diego's **Bali Hai** and **Kona Kai**, Los Angeles's **Tonga Hut** (1958) and **Kelbo's**, San Francisco's **Tiki Bob's**, and the bars in the Don the Beachcomber and Trader Vic's chains.

### 4.4 Disney's Enchanted Tiki Room (1963)

On **23 June 1963**, Disneyland opened **Walt Disney's Enchanted Tiki Room**, a show of singing birds, flowers and carved tiki gods. It was the first attraction built around Disney's Audio-Animatronics. The show brought the tiki style to children and families and fixed the image of the singing, drumming tiki in popular culture [35][44].

### 4.5 Architecture and design: "Tiki Modern"

Polynesian Pop became a real architectural movement, which Sven Kirsten documented as **Tiki Modern** [45]:
- **A-frames and "long-house" gables** with swooping, steeply pitched rooflines, modeled loosely on Sepik River spirit houses and Polynesian meeting houses.
- **Tiki motels and apartment complexes**, especially in Southern California, Arizona and Florida. They had lava-rock walls, torches, carved poles and names like "Kona Kai," "Islander" and "Royal Hawaiian." Palm Springs examples such as the Caliente Tropics motel are still admired.
- Bowling alleys, supper clubs and even gas stations with tiki touches.

The décor industry grew with it:
- **Oceanic Arts** of Whittier, California, founded in **1956** by **LeRoy Schmaltz** and **Bob Van Oosting**, carved tikis and supplied thatch, bamboo, floats, lamps and masks to restaurants and theme parks, including Disney, for more than six decades [44].
- **Witco**, a Seattle maker of dark, deeply carved wood furniture and wall art, sold "tiki modern" style to homes and hotels in the 1950s–70s [45].
- **Velvet paintings** of island women were collected and hung in bars. The best known were by Edgar Leeteg, the American painter who lived in Tahiti.
- **Glass fishing floats, puffer-fish lamps, tapa cloth, outrigger canoes and nets** became standard décor.

### 4.6 Mugs and vessels

The **ceramic tiki mug**, shaped like a tiki god, a skull, a coconut, a Moai head or a barrel, became the style's signature object and later its main collectible [44]:
- Early **house mugs** were made for specific restaurants such as Trader Vic's, Don the Beachcomber, the Mai-Kai and Kahiki.
- Importers and suppliers such as **Otagiri Mercantile Co. (OMC)**, Japanese-made mugs for the U.S. market, and **Orchids of Hawaii**, a supplier of island-themed barware, produced catalog mugs and bowls sold to hundreds of restaurants and to the public. That is why the same mug designs turn up in bars across the country [44].
- **Coconut and pineapple shells**, **Scorpion and Volcano bowls** with center wells for flaming rum, and the **ceramic Fu Manchu and "Chinaman" mugs** that are now recognized as offensive all came from this period.

> **Contested: the "first tiki mug."** Several objects are put forward as the first true tiki mug, including early Trader Vic's and Tiki Bob's (San Francisco, mid-1950s) pieces. There is no consensus [44].

### 4.7 Exotica music

Polynesian Pop had its own soundtrack, **Exotica** [35][44]:
- **Les Baxter**'s ***Ritual of the Savage* (1951)** set the template with lush orchestral "jungle" pieces, including "Quiet Village."
- **Martin Denny**, playing at the Shell Bar of Henry Kaiser's Hawaiian Village hotel in Waikiki, recorded "Quiet Village" with a small combo whose players imitated **bird calls and frog croaks**. The croaks began as a joke inspired by real frogs outside the bar. Denny's album ***Exotica* (1957)** went to No. 1 in **1959**.
- **Arthur Lyman**, Denny's former vibraphonist, launched his own career (*Taboo*, 1958).

Hawaiian steel guitar, the long-running *Hawaii Calls* radio show and Elvis Presley's film ***Blue Hawaii* (1961)** filled out the sound.

### 4.8 The Caribbean resort line

Tropical drinks were also coming out of Caribbean and Gulf Coast resorts, and these matter as much as the tiki temples for how people drink today:

- **Piña Colada (Puerto Rico).**
  > **Contested:** The **Caribe Hilton** in San Juan credits bartender **Ramón "Monchito" Marrero** with creating it in **1954**. Another Caribe Hilton bartender, **Ricardo García**, also claimed it. The **Barrachina** restaurant in Old San Juan claims its bartender **Ramón Portas Mingot** created it in **1963**. Earlier Cuban travel writing uses "piña colada" (strained pineapple) for a pineapple drink sometimes spiked with rum. The modern drink depended on **Coco López** cream of coconut, developed in Puerto Rico around the late 1940s and early 1950s. Puerto Rico named the Piña Colada its official drink in **1978** [42].
- **Hurricane (New Orleans).** Pat O'Brien's bar is credited with the passion-fruit-and-rum Hurricane in the 1940s, reputedly created to use up rum that distributors forced on bars. It shares its name, but probably not its recipe, with the 1939 World's Fair "Hurricane" bar where the Zombie was poured [10][42].
- **Painkiller (British Virgin Islands).** Created in the 1970s at the Soggy Dollar Bar on Jost Van Dyke and later trademarked by Pusser's rum [42].
- **Jungle Bird (1973, Kuala Lumpur).** Created by Jeffrey Ong at the Aviary Bar as the Kuala Lumpur Hilton's opening welcome drink in July 1973. It was long dated to 1978, from the first printed recipe, and was later revived by Berry (*Intoxica!*, 2002) and craft bartenders [39].

## 5. Decline (c. 1968–1990)

Polynesian Pop faded from the late 1960s and was deeply unfashionable by the 1980s. Several causes worked together [35][44]:

1. **Generational rejection.** To the counterculture and the young adults of the late 1960s and 1970s, tiki was their parents' suburban kitsch, part of the cocktail-party world they were rejecting.
2. **The Vietnam era.** The romantic WWII image of the Pacific as paradise gave way to images of war in Southeast Asia. The escapist, colonial fantasy of "the Orient" and "the South Seas" became harder to sell and easier to mock.
3. **Pre-mixes, sour mix and cost-cutting.** As bars scaled up and experienced bartenders retired, fresh juice gave way to **bottled sweet-and-sour mix**, artificial grenadine, canned juices and cheaper rum. Proprietary "Mai Tai mixes" (including Vic's own) made the drink easy to fake. Frozen blender drinks in resort bars pushed the style toward **sweet, slushy, low-quality** drinks [43].
4. **The "umbrella drink" reputation.** By the 1970s–80s, a "tiki drink" meant a sugary cocktail with a paper umbrella and a maraschino cherry, a vacation joke rather than a serious drink. The success of the Piña Colada, capped by Rupert Holmes's 1979 hit "Escape (The Piña Colada Song)," put the tropical cocktail at its most commercial and least respected.
5. **New bar formats.** **Fern bars** such as Henry Africa's in San Francisco (1970), with hanging plants, Tiffany-style lamps and sweet drinks, and later singles bars and chain "casual dining" took over the market for fun nights out.
6. **Real estate and redevelopment.** Big, costly Polynesian palaces on valuable land were closed or demolished as leases ended and owners retired. The Luau closed in the late 1970s. Stephen Crane's Kon-Tiki rooms disappeared one by one. The mainland Don the Beachcomber chain shrank away [44].

Some institutions survived: Trader Vic's, by internationalizing; the Mai-Kai, the Tonga Room, the Tiki-Ti and the Kahiki (until 2000), by holding on. Their survival turned out to be important, because they kept original recipes and rooms intact for the revival [40][44].

## 6. Revival (1990s–2010s)

### 6.1 Lounge culture and "urban archaeology"

The revival began among **collectors, musicians and design enthusiasts**, not bartenders [44]:
- In the early–mid 1990s, a **lounge and exotica revival** reissued Martin Denny and Les Baxter records, and a retro swing and cocktail scene rediscovered mid-century style.
- Thrift stores and, from the mid-1990s, eBay made tiki mugs and décor cheap and collectible.
- **Otto von Stroheim** founded the fanzine ***Tiki News* in 1995**, connecting a scattered scene. He went on to found **Tiki Oasis**, a weekender that started in the early 2000s and later settled in San Diego. It became the scene's biggest annual gathering.
- **Sven A. Kirsten**, a Los Angeles filmmaker and self-described "urban archaeologist," published ***The Book of Tiki* (Taschen, 2000)**. It named and catalogued the whole movement, gave it an art-historical frame, and made "tiki" the accepted umbrella term. He followed with *Tiki Modern* (2007) and *Tiki Pop* (2014) [44][45].
- Online forums, especially **Tiki Central**, linked collectors, carvers and home bartenders [27].

### 6.2 Jeff "Beachbum" Berry: recovering the recipes

Collectors had the mugs; nobody had the real recipes. **Jeff Berry**, a Los Angeles screenwriter turned drinks historian, spent years tracking down retired bartenders, their notebooks and their families. His books rebuilt the canon:
- ***Beachbum Berry's Grog Log* (1998)** [38];
- ***Intoxica!* (2002)**, which brought obscure drinks such as the Jungle Bird to light [39];
- ***Taboo Table* (2005)**, a food book;
- ***Sippin' Safari* (2007)**, with the **1934 Zombie** decoded from the Santiago notebook, profiles of the Filipino and Hawaiian bartenders, and the Don's Mix/Spices decodings [8][40];
- ***Beachbum Berry Remixed* (2010)**, which revised and combined the first two books [41];
- ***Potions of the Caribbean* (2013)**, on the Caribbean roots of the style [42].

In **2014** Berry and his wife Annene Kaye opened **Latitude 29** in New Orleans's French Quarter, a working bar built on his research. Berry has also lent his name and palate to products that fill old gaps, most notably a **Zombie rum blend** made with Ed Hamilton in **2021** [9][37].

### 6.3 The craft cocktail movement meets tiki

From the late 1980s, bartenders such as **Dale DeGroff** at New York's Rainbow Room brought back **fresh juice, measured pours and classic recipes**. By the mid-2000s, craft bars such as Milk & Honey, Pegu Club and Death & Co had made pre-Prohibition cocktails respectable again. Tiki drinks were the next logical step. They are demanding recipes with many ingredients that only work with fresh citrus, good rum and careful dilution. Craft bartenders embraced them as a technical challenge [43].

Several things aligned:
- **Better rum and ingredients** reached the U.S. market: navy-strength Jamaican pot-still rum (Smith & Cross, 2009), rhum agricole, Ed Hamilton's imports, St. Elizabeth Allspice Dram (2008), Velvet Falernum, and small-batch orgeat and passion-fruit syrups (see [`concepts.md`](concepts.md)).
- **Berry's decoded specs** gave bartenders an accurate canon to work from.
- **Tales of the Cocktail** in New Orleans and similar events gave tiki a professional stage.

### 6.4 Martin Cate and Smuggler's Cove

**Martin Cate** worked at Trader Vic's in Emeryville before opening his own bars:
- **Forbidden Island** (Alameda, **2006**);
- **Smuggler's Cove** (San Francisco, **December 2009**), a multi-level bar with one of the largest rum collections in the U.S. and a menu that mixes Berry-decoded classics, Caribbean punches and new drinks.

Smuggler's Cove became the model for the modern tiki bar: museum-quality décor, strict fresh-juice technique, and education about rum [43]. Cate later took part in other projects, among them Hale Pele (Portland) and False Idol (San Diego).

In **2016**, Martin and Rebecca Cate published ***Smuggler's Cove: Exotic Cocktails, Rum, and the Cult of Tiki***. It combined history, bar-building and more than a hundred recipes. It also introduced a **rum classification** based on **production method (pot, column, blended, cane juice) and age**, with categories such as "blended aged," "pot still unaged," "black blended overproof" and "cane AOC Martinique rhum agricole." Recipes call for a *category*, not a brand, so readers can make them with whatever good rum is available [43]. The book won major industry awards and became the standard reference for the revival.

### 6.5 The new temples

The 2010s brought ambitious tiki bars back to major cities, including:
- **Three Dots and a Dash** (Chicago, **2013**), a basement bar created by **Paul McGee** with the Hogsalt group, named for Donn's wartime drink;
- **Lost Lake** (Chicago, **2015**), from McGee and **Shelby Allison**, which leaned brighter and less kitsch;
- **Latitude 29** (New Orleans, 2014);
- **Hale Pele** (Portland), **False Idol** (San Diego), **Pagan Idol** (San Francisco), **Trader Sam's** at the Disneyland Hotel, and many more.

The surviving classic rooms, including the Tiki-Ti, the Tonga Room, the Mai-Kai and the Trader Vic's in Emeryville, became places of pilgrimage [40][43].

## 7. The present: reckoning and "tropical"

### 7.1 The appropriation debate

As tiki became fashionable again in the 2010s, people inside and outside the drinks industry began to question its imagery. The main criticisms, raised by Pacific Islander writers and artists, academics, and bartenders, include [51][52][54][55]:

- **Sacred imagery as décor.** Carved figures descend from representations of gods and ancestors (Māori, Marquesan, Hawaiian *kiʻi*). Using them as bar mascots, drinking vessels and punchlines trivializes living religious and cultural traditions.
- **A flattened, invented "Polynesia."** Tiki merges distinct Pacific cultures into one generic "island" look. Most of it was designed by non-Islanders for non-Islanders.
- **Sexualized and "savage" stereotypes.** The hula girl, the grass skirt, the cannibal and the headhunter are colonial tropes. Hawaiian scholars such as **Haunani-Kay Trask** (*From a Native Daughter*, 1993) and **Adria Imada** (*Aloha America*, 2012) have analyzed how tourism turned Hawaiian women and hula into products [54][55].
- **Colonial and military context.** The fantasy grew alongside the U.S. overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom (1893), annexation, militarization and WWII. Some critics also point to period "Oriental" mugs and caricatures of Asian people.
- **Erased labor.** The famous names are white restaurateurs. Much of the actual bartending was done by **Filipino, Chinese-American and Caribbean** workers whose recipes were kept secret and who went uncredited [40][51].

### 7.2 "Tropical" as a reframing

One response has been to separate **the drinks** (a Caribbean-rooted rum tradition with serious technique) from **the Polynesian iconography**:

- **Garret Richard and Ben Wald**, in ***Tropical Standard: Cocktail Techniques & Reinvented Recipes* (2023)**, deliberately use "tropical" rather than "tiki." They build a modern technical approach to the genre, with precise sugar and acid, shelf-stable versions of classic mixes (a Don's Mix made from grapefruit and cinnamon extracts at the classic 2:1 ratio, for example [13]), and systematic rum substitution. They also question the old imagery. Richard's bar work, notably at Brooklyn's **Sunken Harbor Club**, shows the approach in practice [13][51].
- **Shannon Mustipher**, a Brooklyn rum specialist, emphasizes the drinks' **Caribbean roots** and a diverse view of the rum world in ***Tiki: Modern Tropical Cocktails* (2019)** [52].
- **Pacific Islander perspectives** in articles, panels and social media push for real consultation, for Islander artists and staff, and for the removal of the most offensive imagery. Views differ among Pacific Islanders themselves. Some object to any tiki theming; others accept a respectful, clearly fictional style or embrace the drinks while rejecting the idols [35][54].

### 7.3 How bars are responding

In the early 2020s, several responses have become common (no single approach dominates):
- dropping sacred-figure mugs, "savage" names and hula-girl imagery;
- rebranding as "tropical," "rum bar," "exotic" or nautical rather than "tiki";
- **commissioning Pacific Islander artists** and crediting sources;
- telling the story of the **Filipino and Caribbean bartenders** and the Caribbean rum industry on menus and in staff training;
- keeping the escapist design (plants, bamboo, rattan, water, lighting, music) while dropping the ethnic caricature.

Related changes have reached the rum industry. **Plantation rum** was renamed **Planteray** (announced 2020, new name revealed 2023, rolled out 2024) after criticism of the word's link to slavery (see [`concepts.md`](concepts.md)).

Other present-day threads:
- the **Mai-Kai's** restoration and reopening;
- the continued rise of **rum education**, including debates over added sugar and additives and the Gargano/Seale production-based classification;
- the use of **modern techniques** (clarification, acid adjustment, stable syrups) in tropical drinks [43][51].

Tiki has survived by changing: from Hollywood speakeasy, to chain restaurant, to kitsch, to connoisseur's craft, and now to a style that has to justify its imagery.

## 8. Themes and aesthetics

Across nine decades, the same design ideas recur. They are part of what a "tiki drink" is, and a generator of new drinks should understand them.

### 8.1 Escapism

The main purpose is **transport**: a room with no windows, low light, the sound of water, and drinks with names from an imaginary map. Donn's rain-on-the-roof effect, the Tonga Room's storms, the Mai-Kai's gardens and Kahiki's rain forest all serve this one idea [2][44]. Drinks are named for the same purpose:
- **danger and the supernatural:** Zombie, Cobra's Fang, Shark's Tooth, Dr. Funk;
- **the colonial adventure story:** Missionary's Downfall, Planter's Punch, Test Pilot, Suffering Bastard;
- **pseudo-Polynesian phrases:** Mai Tai, Nui Nui, Aku Aku, Pupule;
- **place:** Tahitian Rum Punch, Barbados Red Rum Swizzle.

### 8.2 Theater

- **Fire:** flaming lime shells or sugar cubes soaked in 151-proof rum, Volcano bowls with a burning center well, and the "Mystery Drink" rituals with gong and dancer.
- **Smoke and fog:** dry-ice effects in revival bars.
- **Scale and communal drinking:** Scorpion bowls, Volcano bowls and punch bowls with long straws for two to eight people, a direct descendant of the 17th-century punch bowl.
- **Vessels as characters:** skulls, tikis, coconuts, pineapples, Moai.
- **The ice cone:** Donn's Navy Grog was served around a cone of shaved ice molded around the straw.

### 8.3 Garnish vocabulary

Garnish is part of how a tiki drink tastes, smells and reads. The standard vocabulary:

| Garnish | Function and history |
|---|---|
| **Mint sprig or bouquet** | Aroma at the nose, especially on crushed-ice drinks (Mai Tai, Zombie); the Mai Tai's mint was meant to suggest a palm |
| **Spent lime shell** | The squeezed half-lime left in the drink (Mai Tai), sometimes flipped into a "boat" and **flamed** with a sugar cube or 151 |
| **Pineapple wedge or fronds** | Tropical signal plus aroma; the fronds give height |
| **Orchid** (dendrobium) | Popularized by Hawaii resort bartenders (Harry Yee); edible but mostly visual |
| **Maraschino cherry** | Color accent; "cherry and pineapple flag" skewers |
| **Paper parasol** | 1950s Hawaii resort era; the emblem of the "umbrella drink" |
| **Grated nutmeg and cinnamon** | Aromatic spice, carried over from punch (Planter's Punch, Painkiller) |
| **Sugar-cane stick** | Stirrer and visual reference to rum's origin |
| **Swizzle stick** (bois lélé) | The Caribbean tool that gave the "swizzle" its name |
| **Edible flowers, dehydrated citrus, cocktail picks** | Revival and modern additions |

### 8.4 Glassware and ice

- **Tall "chimney" or Zombie glass:** Zombie, Test Pilot.
- **Double old-fashioned:** Mai Tai.
- **Pilsner and hurricane glasses:** Navy Grog, Hurricane.
- **Ceramic mugs, coconuts and pineapples:** house specialties.
- **Bowls:** Scorpion, Volcano, Fog Cutter.
- **Ice:** crushed, shaved and pebble ice rather than cubes, both for the look (frosty, overflowing) and for fast chilling and dilution in hot climates.

### 8.5 Décor and music

- **Materials:** bamboo, rattan and wicker, lauhala matting, thatch, tapa cloth, lava rock, puffer-fish lamps, glass floats, nets, outrigger canoes and ship's gear, carved posts, velvet paintings, and running water.
- **Sound:** exotica (Denny, Baxter, Lyman), Hawaiian steel guitar and slack-key, and, in the revival, surf rock and new exotica bands.
- **Mood:** dark rooms lit by warm, colored, indirect light, private booths, and the sense that time has stopped.
